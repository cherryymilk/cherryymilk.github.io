### Introduction

In early 2025, residents of Kōtō City in downtown Tokyo found their neighborhood suddenly awash with tourists. These visitors flocked to a local subway station, Kiyosumi-Shirakawa Station, drawn by the hunt for ‘Exit 8’—an exit that, as it turns out, does not actually exist at the station, which counts only five accessways (Shueisha Online Editorial News Team 2025). The locals were surprised to discover that a station they passed through every day had become the site of rumored paranormal activity following the international success of Kotake Create’s *The Exit 8*, a 2023 Japanese horror video game set within the confines of a sterile, infinitely looping subway corridor, whose eerie environment was said to have been modeled after Kiyosumi-Shirakawa (Koseki 2025). Seemingly overnight, the station had become an urban legend and ‘holy place’ for horror enthusiasts, fueled in no small part by the announcement of a film adaptation of the game set to release later that year.

That a low-budget indie title managed to generate such remarkable popular interest makes it all the more surprising that *Exit 8* has thus far entirely escaped the attention of literary scholarship. With this essay, I seek to address this gap through a close reading of both the *Exit 8* video game and Genki Kawamura’s 2025 film adaptation[^1]. These works merit closer literary analysis, not least because they stand at the intersection of several meaningful contemporary currents in popular culture. Most immediately, the *Exit 8* setting is aesthetically aligned with what has become known in online discourse as *liminal spaces*: strangely desolate locations connoting the “in-between, neither before nor after,” which “stimulate feelings of the uncanny and evoke an overall creepy aesthetic” (Wiggins 2025, 4731–32). This has become a recurring aesthetic across cultural products in recent years and has drawn much critical interest as a result. Likewise, Kawamura’s adaptation emerges amid what has increasingly been described as a “golden age of video game adaptations” (Truskett 2026). Crucially, this is a trend has recently expanded beyond blockbuster franchises to encompass indie horror titles, challenging the longstanding assumption that such properties are commercially unviable for adaptation (Stefansky 2026). This is emblematized by productions like *The Cuphead Show!* (2022), the *Five Nights at Freddy’s* films (2023–2025), Mark Fischbach’s *Iron Lung* (2026), and, indeed, the *Exit 8* film adaptation.

It is a third, largely overlooked cultural groundswell that bears most directly on the present essay, namely what Schniedermann et al. have identified as “a notable increase in temporal loops in both mainstream and experimental storytelling in recent years” (2025, 1). In fact, the *Exit 8* game and film both participate in a flourishing tradition of ‘time loop narratives’ across genre and media, in which a character’s temporal experience within the story is cyclical (see Richardson 2025). The model is no doubt Harold Ramis’s *Groundhog Day* (1993), a film now so culturally entrenched that its title alone signals looping temporality, but the motif extends far beyond any single text and pervades today’s cultural production. It is this contemporary vogue for time loop narratives that opens onto the formal and ideological questions with which this essay is concerned. Indeed, I set out to analyze the *Exit 8* video game and its film adaptation by, following Marco Caracciolo, “reading for the loop” (2025, 153). This entails taking seriously the loop as a versatile and transposable form (in a new formalist sense; see Levine 2015) with affordances that extend beyond the aesthetic into the social and political domains.

Against this backdrop, I argue that the *Exit 8* video game closely intertwines its gameplay and minimal narrative by organizing both around isomorphic looping structures: the gameplay loop and the time loop. Because progress through these loops depends upon the player’s ability to discern minute environmental details that are easily passed over, the game compels a mode of play that favors attentiveness over efficiency or mastery. Genki Kawamura’s film adaptation, in turn, inherits this formal logic and charges it with ideological meaning, using the loop to stage the cyclical unfreedom of capitalist modernity and its foreclosure of futurity. Yet this is not where its primary investment lies. Rather, drawing on Hannah Arendt’s theorization of labor and action in *The Human Condition* (\[1958\] 2018), I argue that the film reinterprets the game’s criteria for escaping the loop as a way of thinking through how futures beyond the cyclical present might once again become imaginable. It is in this sense that the loop becomes, as Caracciolo suggests, a form uniquely attuned to the politics of futurity, to “how different the future might look depending on our … actions in the present” (2025, 154).

This essay proceeds in three parts. I begin by briefly situating *Exit 8* within discussions of the relationship between ludic and narrative loops. I then analyze the formal logic of the video game, before turning to how that same logic acquires ideological significance in the film adaptation.

### Gameplay Loops and Time Loops

Cyclicality and iteration are part and parcel of gameplay. Game design scholars have long recognized that one of the medium’s most foundational structures is the *positive feedback loop*, i.e., a self-reinforcing cycle in which outputs are reintroduced as inputs, amplifying the player’s advantage over time (Salen and Zimmerman 2004, 215). It is often found on the level of mechanics, for instance when the game progressively rewards the player with advantages (e.g., items, abilities, modifiers) that make gameplay easier (see Adams 2010, 349). Crucially, though, this structure is also present on the level of player experience, where earlier insights flow back into and gradually refine the player’s command of the ludic system. Jesper Juul has argued that it is above all this “experience of learning, of adjusting strategies, of trying something new” (2009, 250)— a feedback loop of failure and growth—that makes games appealing. Underlying both the mechanical and experiential feedback loops of video games, however, is a still more fundamental cyclical structure, namely the *gameplay loop*. I follow Miguel Sicart in defining the gameplay loop as “a composite of game mechanics, computing operations, and feedback mechanisms that is repeated until a break condition is reached” (2015). Though this loop is present in virtually every game, it can manifest in various ways, some revolving around a singular loop with basic instructions (e.g., *Tetris*) while others arrange a complex system of interconnected gameplay loops (e.g., *Civilization*).

What warrants my attention to the cyclical structures of video games is their resemblance to the temporal loops found in narrative. One way to conceptualize the time loop, according to Linda Lahdenperä, is as a form that manipulates a narrative’s temporal unfolding in such a way that it imitates and remediates “the replay quality of video games” (2018, 144; see also Bolter and Grusin 1999). Schniedermann et al. have likewise pointed out that time loop narratives tend to feature characters who accrue and retain information with each iteration of the loop (2025, 4), serving as a narrative equivalent of the positive feedback loop that defines player experience in video games. Their affinities notwithstanding, these structures remain on separate ontological levels,  since the ludic loops of video games are mechanical and hence extradiegetic (*syuzhet*) whereas narrative time loops are diegetic (*fabula*). As Lahdenperä has noted, however, there is a unique case in which loop-based structures operate on both levels at once; i.e., “when a video game has a time loop narrative” (2018, 150). These games, for instance *Outer Wilds* (2019), *Returnal* (2021), and *12 Minutes* (2021), project their ludic cyclicality onto the diegesis via the time loop motif, creating what can be seen as a *mise en abyme* of loop-based structures. Put simply, they “narrativize … elements like repetition and failure that are frequently part of play but outside of a game’s storyworld” (Barkman 2022). It is this same dynamic that, I argue, is at play in *Exit 8*.

### Escaping the Loop: The *Exit 8* Video Game

Beneath the liminal aesthetics that characterize *Exit 8* lies a remarkably straightforward gameplay loop. Upon launching the game, the player finds herself[^2] at one end of a deserted underground corridor illuminated by fluorescent lights. The corridor loops back on itself, returning the player to where she started with every turn of the corner. The only other presence in the corridor is a man in business attire carrying a briefcase, who walks past in the opposite direction with each loop, never acknowledging the player. There are rules posted on a sign next to her: “Don’t overlook any anomalies / If you find anomalies, turn back immediately / If you don’t find anomalies, do not turn back / To go out from Exit 8” (Kotake Create 2023). In essence, the gameplay thus comes down to a spot-the-difference exercise: with each new iteration of the loop, the player must determine whether the corridor is different from before, with anomalies ranging from subtle changes (e.g., altered advertisement posters) to more overtly uncanny apparitions (e.g., a flood of mysterious red liquid). In that sense, *Exit 8* is not dissimilar to games like *I’m on Observation Duty* (2018), *That’s Not My Neighbor* (2024), and *The Cabin Factory* (2024), which together can be said to constitute a nascent subgenre of ‘anomaly hunting’ horror that bridges “the gap between ‘liminal space horror’ and ‘spot-the-difference puzzle’” (Second Wind 2025).

On account of its simple gameplay loop, *Exit 8* has also come to be regarded as a quintessential example of what is (rather controversially) called the *walking simulator* (see Zimmerman and Huberts 2019). Despite the pejorative origins of this label (see Ruberg 2020), Hugo Montembeault and Maxime Deslongchamps-Gagnon have surveyed themes associated with the walking simulator on the game distribution platform Steam and found that the genre typically revolves around qualities like ‘exploration,’ ‘narration,’ ‘storytelling,’ an ‘open world,’ and a ‘great soundtrack’ (2019). As *Exit 8* adopts only a handful of these narrative conventions, however, I argue against its designation as a prototypical walking simulator. The game is much more elementary than many of its generic counterparts, pairing its ludic mechanics with just one narrative technique, namely the unsettling atmosphere of its looping, shifting setting. There is no narration or soundtrack to speak of, no wider world waiting to be explored. *Exit 8* is, in essence, about looking carefully at the same claustrophobic environment over and over again.

This seems to be a stumbling block for some players: one reviewer on Steam considers the game wanting in “anything that could make \[it\] interesting,” while another writes that it “barely seems like a game” at all (Cimroa 2025; Zer0_Ph34r 2026). These responses are hardly surprising given the broader counter-hegemonic nature of walking simulators (Bowman 2019, 152; see Galloway 2006 on *countergaming*), but I want to make a case for *Exit 8*’s simplicity as a merit rather than a shortcoming, even by the genre’s own standards. That the game pares down the walking simulator is precisely what renders it significant: this minimalism brings the isomorphy of its ludic and diegetic loops to the fore, making the game a uniquely exemplary instance of the *mise en abyme* described above. The gameplay loop and time loop converge almost entirely in *Exit 8*, as the game’s diegesis offers little beyond its looping environment that could set it apart from the ludic level. These structures can thus, drawing on Dan Pinchbeck’s terminology, be called *ludodiegetic*, in that they are so integrated as to be functionally inseparable (2007, 12). Further still, the distance between the player and the character she embodies closes in equal measure, as no diegetic material (name, interiority, appearance, etc.) individuates the latter beyond their function as an *avatar* for the player to manipulate (see Klevjer 2022; Bayliss 2007). Without such material to mediate the player’s relationship to the time loop, its constraints—i.e., endless repetition and the risk of failure—bear directly on her rather than being filtered through a character’s experience. In the conventional (involuntary) time loop narrative, as per Schniedermann, a “key thematic function … lies in generating a search for meaning for both protagonist and audience” (2023, 299). *Exit 8*, however, eliminates the protagonist as a diegetic intermediary altogether, inviting us to locate meaning in what the loop demands of the player herself.

What becomes most salient in this regard, to reiterate Sicart’s terminology, is the ludodiegetic loop’s *break condition* (2015). In effect, putting an end to the loop is “the ultimate, nonnegotiable goal” of any involuntary time loop narrative (Schniedermann 2023, 301). However, where the protagonist’s quest to ascertain the loop’s break condition is typically central to the plot of these narratives, *Exit 8* reveals this condition from the outset. The player must, with each loop, accurately determine whether or not her environment harbors an anomaly. If she makes eight correct assessments consecutively, the time loop—and with it the game loop—comes to an end, revealing an ascending flight of stairs with light filtering down from the surface. Any error resets her progress entirely. What the break condition demands from the player, then, is for her to adopt a mode of play that is counter-hegemonic insofar as it transgresses what Janine Fron et al. term the *hegemony of play*, i.e., the “power structures \[… of\] game technologies, game production and game consumption” that “perpetuate a particular set of values and norms” and drive “the critical discourse of what is and is not a game”(2007, 310). Whereas hegemonic play is organized around “intellectual problem-solving and resource management” wherein the player must typically “amass armies, expand territories, control resources, and dominate the play space” as difficulty escalates and pace quickens (Fullerton et al. 2008), *Exit 8* makes advancement contingent upon the player’s willingness to slow down, linger, observe, and play unproductively. Indeed, the game’s subtler anomalies totally resist hegemonic play, leading one reviewer to write that they “REALLY wanted to enjoy” the game but “ended up just frustrated” with having to spot “the most miniscule of differences” (Rob 2026).

In this sense, the mode of play demanded by *Exit 8*’s break condition brings to mind what John Vanderhoef and Matthew Thomas Payne have termed *slow game time* (2022), though I would argue that the value of this slowed pace lies primarily in how it reshapes the player’s relationship to her *environment* and, in doing so, reorients her attention toward details that would otherwise pass unnoticed. Alenda Y. Chang, writing from an ecocritical perspective, has argued that the environment typically “remains inert” in video games, with action taking place “within or in front of such digital set pieces” and “the range of possible interaction with … game environments remain\[ing\] disappointingly slight” (2019, 21–22). In *Exit 8*, by contrast, environmental minutiae such as posters, signs, and architectural features move from the backdrop of gameplay to its primary focus, as these become charged with ludic meaning as potential sites of anomaly. Since progression is altogether contingent upon the player’s engagement with these “set pieces,” she is compelled to adopt a counter-hegemonic mode of *attentiveness* to minute deviations within an otherwise repetitive environment. This renders the loop’s instability—its imperfect repetition across resets in the form of anomalies (see Schniedermann 2023)—a condition that opens onto an escape from its sameness. The break condition that characterizes gameplay in *Exit 8* thus intimates that cyclical structures are not so total as to foreclose the possibility of change, and that change might be effected by attending to where they crack.

### Escaping the Present: The *Exit 8* Film Adaptation

It is in Genki Kawamura’s 2025 film adaptation of *Exit 8* that the affordances of the loop, and especially of its break condition, are given ideological significance. Much like the video game, it follows an unnamed man who is trapped in a looping subway corridor and must identify a series of anomalies to escape. What sets the film apart, though, is that it moves away from the game’s radical minimalism by furnishing the storyworld with new diegetic material, including a psychologized protagonist, supporting characters, and a social world before and after the loop. This allows the film to develop thematic concerns that remain implicit in the game, inviting us to interpret the loop beyond its immediate ludic and narrative functions as, following Levine, a form whose affordances operate across the aesthetic, social, and political domains (2015). This portability renders the loop ideologically significant: as Caracciolo has convincingly argued, “form is never neutral” and is in fact “responsible, together with its thematic dimension, for the negotiation of cultural issues,” carrying “ideological implications that should be carefully unpacked” (2024, 370, 358). The question, then, is what ideological work the loop performs in Kawamura’s film.

On one level, the film uses the time loop to stage a *Bildungsroman* of sorts, facilitating the protagonist’s moral transformation from an indecisive and indifferent man to one who, after breaking the loop, assumes social responsibility in both the private and public sphere. The narrative is bookended by identical scenes: during his commute, the protagonist witnesses a man berating a young mother and her crying baby before he receives a call from his ex-girlfriend, who informs him that she is pregnant. Where he initially fails to defend the mother and cannot bring himself to answer over the phone, he exits the loop a changed man, this time stepping in to confront the rude passenger and calling his ex-girlfriend, promising to meet her at the hospital. More fundamentally, though, I contend that the *Exit 8* film adaptation uses the time loop as a narrative strategy through which the protagonist learns to recognize the cyclical structures that organize his life under capitalist modernity and, ultimately, to act against them. In a *Guardian* review, Peter Bradshaw reads working-class existence in the world of *Exit 8* as an “elegant, chilly dream of despair,” noting that

\[a\]ll these wage-slave commuters on the metro believe in the game of life, taking the blue pill, doing the same thing every day, completing the levels of their professional careers; trusting that the rules, though fiendishly difficult, are fair on their own terms. But the young man can’t get out. Is his nightmarish paralysis a parable for expectant-father anxiety? Maybe. But ... \[i\]t is crushing just taking place in featureless modern buildings ... whose forms insist on our anonymity and insignificance (2026).

This reading is suggestive insofar as it locates Kawamura’s critique in the repetitive rhythms of capitalism’s “game of life” and the “nightmarish paralysis” they produce. Caracciolo has argued that the loop is a narrative form uniquely suited for representing just this paralysis, as “\[i\]ts disruption of narrative linearity mirrors our inability to see the future as a mere extension of the present” (2025, 154). In this view, by trapping the protagonist within a seemingly inescapable temporal cycle that endlessly reproduces the present and forecloses alternative futures, the time loop can be said to literalize what Mark Fisher has termed ‘capitalist realism’: “the widespread sense that not only is capitalism the only viable political and economic system, but also that it is now impossible even to *imagine* a coherent alternative to it” (2009, 2).

What Bradshaw fails to acknowledge, however, is that the protagonist *does* break the loop in the end. As a result, his interpretation overlooks what I take to be the film’s central stake, namely futurity itself. Indeed, I argue that Kawamura’s use of the time loop motif goes beyond diagnosing the foreclosure of alternative futures under capitalist modernity to imagining how this foreclosure might be *overcome* through the loop’s break condition. Its investment is thus, crucially, in modeling what it would take to break with the untenable present, not what the future might look like. To interpret how the film approaches this issue, I turn to Hannah Arendt’s *The Human Condition*, which draws a tripartite distinction of “fundamental human activities”: *work*, *labor*, and *action* (2018, 7). Arendt’s theorization is particularly valuable here, as it productively complements Fisher’s in allowing us to think through how a break from capitalist realism might be imagined on the level of individual behavior—here, the protagonist’s.

Particularly salient are Arendt’s categories of labor and action. Labor, governed by necessity, refers to those activities that have as their sole end the maintenance of life and which, unlike work, do not produce durable artifacts. Eating, sleeping, and washing are forms of labor in this sense, as they must be undertaken again and again throughout the course of life and yield no lasting result. These activities are therefore “endlessly repetitive,” taking the form of a loop: as Arendt writes, “all human activities which arise out of the necessity to cope with them are bound to the recurring cycles of nature and have in themselves no beginning and no end … *laboring* always moves in the same circle” (2018, 98). Action, by contrast, “is not forced upon us by necessity” but rather denotes the faculty to “take an initiative, to begin …, to set something in motion” (177). Speaking out against an injustice or making a promise are instances of action, insofar as they set into motion a course of events that escapes the logic of the loop and whose outcomes are not entirely predictable. For Arendt, action is therefore the “actualization of the human condition of *natality*,” that is, the uniquely human capacity to begin something new, to “perform what is infinitely improbable” (178, my emphasis).

Arendt uses this distinction to argue that modern society increasingly organizes human life according to labor’s looping temporality at the expense of action and the natality it actualizes. She writes that the modern age has “carried with it a theoretical glorification of labor” and thereby ushered in a “striking new arrangement of hierarchical order … where \[labor\] now \[comes\] to occupy a rank formerly held by political action” (4, 301). Read alongside Fisher, Arendt thus suggests that capitalist realism’s foreclosure of alternative futures is inseparable from a foreclosure of the *possibility* of beginning anew. To break with the present and inaugurate possible futures, then, would be to recover natality from the loop through *action*. I argue that this recovery of natality is precisely what Kawamura’s *Exit 8* demands of its protagonist to end the time loop, using its break condition as a model for how the cyclical unfreedom of capitalist realism might be overcome.

On the surface, the film’s time loop has a break condition that remains unchanged from the video game’s. A sign posted on the subway corridor wall instructs the protagonist: “Do not overlook any anomalies” (Kawamura 2025, 0:13:20). As in the game, his progress depends entirely on how well he can spot deviations in his environment, a task at which he quickly proves rather inept. In relation to the game, I  suggested that successfully identifying anomalies becomes possible only by cultivating a counter-hegemonic mode of attentiveness that brings backgrounded features of the environment into the foreground. This is no less true of Kawamura’s adaptation, though the protagonist fails to recognize this at first and remains beholden to the logic of hegemonic play, seeking the quickest, most efficient means of escaping the loop rather than slowing down to attend to it—which is, ironically, the only way out of the loop. Having read the instructions, he turns not to his environment but to the only other person walking down the corridor—the man in business attire carrying a briefcase—asking: “Excuse me, where is Exit 8?” (0:14:25). Much like in the game, the man remains entirely unresponsive. He then resorts to photographing the corridor using his smartphone, but the files become corrupt with every reset of the loop, prompting an exasperated “What? Why? Why!” (0:18:10). Both examples show how the loop refuses to reward hegemonic play, frustrating the protagonist’s every attempt to circumvent the counter-hegemonic demands of its break condition.

Within the film’s expanded storyworld, this distinction between hegemonic and counter-hegemonic play is, in a Levinean sense, further transposed from the ludic onto the diegetic level, where it comes to define not only the protagonist’s navigation through the loop, but also his attentional priorities in the world beyond it. Before becoming trapped, he shuts his eyes to a young mother’s harassment on the subway and responds evasively to his pregnant ex-girlfriend’s call as he prioritizes making his way to work. Once inside the loop, this same order of priority governs his behavior, as he elevates the goal of reaching Exit 8 over the process of paying attention to the corridor’s anomalies. The correspondence between these behavioral patterns is difficult to overlook, not least because the film itself routinely collapses the boundary between the protagonist’s life outside the loop and his experience within it. Most strikingly, the call from his ex-girlfriend later returns as an anomaly, which suggests a formal equivalence between what the protagonist overlooks beyond the loop and what he overlooks within it. From this, I contend that the film invites us to read the attentional hierarchy of hegemonic play as a model for the attentional hierarchy encouraged by capitalist modernity, in which labor has come to eclipse action. Indeed, read through Arendt, the protagonist’s conduct prior to the loop betrays a disposition in which the cyclical demands of everyday life under capitalism—what Peter Bradshaw referred to earlier as “taking the blue pill, doing the same thing every day” (2026)—take precedence over those occasional interruptions that call for initiative. By making escape from the time loop contingent upon abandoning the logic of hegemonic play, then, the film presents the attentional hierarchy that governs the protagonist’s life beyond the loop as something that can likewise be unlearned.

Notably, the film does not portray this unlearning as an act of individual will on the protagonist’s behalf, but rather as a process made possible through relationality. This is illustrated by the arrival of a young boy in the loop, who proves demonstrably more proficient than the protagonist at spotting anomalies. Immediately following their encounter, the protagonist inattentively presses on through the corridor, whereas the child observes that the exit sign has been altered to read: “Turn Back Turn Back Turn Back” (0:56:22). The protagonist later mistakes an ordinary poster for an anomaly, insisting that it "looks different" and urging the boy to halt: “Wait! It’s an anomaly” (0:58:15). The boy, however, having already inspected the corridor, continues walking and is proven correct: there was no anomaly after all. As the narrative progresses, his relationship with the child thus comes to mediate the protagonist’s acquisition of the counter-hegemonic mode of attentiveness on which escape from the loop depends.

What the film goes on to show, however, is that this new hierarchy of attention is not an end in itself but rather the means through which the protagonist learns to relate differently to those around him. This is reflected in how his relationship with the boy starts to evolve in the course of the narrative. At first, he fails to notice or acknowledge the boy’s talent for spotting anomalies, even snapping at him in irritation when he suspects him of having made an error: “Oh, come on! Now we have to start over again!” (0:58:30). However, this attitude soon gives way to one of trust and care. The protagonist increasingly defers to the boy’s judgment and comes to establish a clear emotional connection with him, asking as they near the exit: “If we get out of here, what will you do?” He smiles when the boy replies in excitement: “Pizza … I want to eat one topped with loads of cheese!” (1:12:20). This growing bond is revealed most clearly when an anomalous liquid floods the corridor, and the protagonist, without hesitation, pulls the child close, firmly grips his hand as they run, and lifts him onto the exit sign to ensure his safety. In analytical terms, the protagonist’s newfound counter-hegemonic attentiveness thus comes to encompass the young boy accompanying him through the loop, showing his growing willingness to subordinate his own interests to those of another. This marks the emergence of what Arendt calls *human plurality*, as the protagonist's conduct reveals that the looping corridor—and therefore the wider world—has become a space he shares with others, rather than one organized around his individual passage toward the exit. As Arendt reminds us, “men, not Man, live on the earth and inhabit the world” (2018, 7).

If, as I have argued, the protagonist’s attentional priorities within the loop serve as a model for his disposition in the wider world, his entry into plurality anticipates a corresponding shift in his responsiveness to others beyond the loop. And indeed, the film immediately bears out this suggestion upon the protagonist’s arrival at Exit 8 and his return to the subway. The deserted corridor at once gives way to the press of bodies in a crowded station, which visually reinforces the protagonist’s Arendtian return to plurality, a world of others. It is within this newly populated world that he now chooses to intervene on the young mother’s behalf and return his ex-girlfriend’s call. These gestures are meaningful because they mark the first true moments in which the protagonist’s reordered attentional hierarchy culminates in *action*, illustrating Arendt’s claim that plurality is the “*conditio per quam* … of human action” (7–8).

Particularly meaningful here is that the protagonist’s actions are directed toward characters associated with pregnancy and childbirth: his expectant ex-girlfriend and the young mother carrying her newborn. This is no coincidence. In fact, there is a clear conceptual association between childbirth and *natality*, one that Arendt herself acknowledges when she writes that natality is “the new beginning inherent in birth” (9). I therefore contend that the ex-girlfriend and the young mother are symbolic vehicles through which the narrative stages the recovery of natality. This recovery is enacted precisely by directing the protagonist’s first actions after escaping the loop toward these symbolic figures. In acting on their behalf, he successfully breaks with the loop of labor and restores the possibility to begin anew, or, to reiterate Arendt, the capacity to “perform what is infinitely improbable” (178).

My synthesis of Arendt and Fisher suggested that natality is essential if the foreclosure of alternative futures under capitalist realism is to be overcome. What the preceding analysis has demonstrated is that *Exit 8* uses the affordances of the time loop to narrativize both this foreclosure *and* the chain of conditions under which natality, and hence alternative futures, might once again become possible. More specifically, the film suggests that the recovery of futurity begins with a reorientation of what is deemed worthy of our attention, as it is only through this initial shift that the protagonist enters into plurality, becomes capable of action, and rediscovers the capacity to begin anew. In this sense, Kawamura’s adaptation can be said to actualize the ideological affordances of *Exit* 8’s ludodiegetic loop. By recasting what functions in the video game as a ludic mechanic into a model of social and political transformation, the film charges the break condition it inherits with ideological meaning, developing it into an account of how new beginnings become possible.

### Conclusion

This essay has argued that the formal organization of Kotake Create’s *Exit 8* renders escape from its ludodiegetic loop contingent upon the player’s willingness to adopt a mode of play that upsets the hegemonic attentional hierarchy of foreground and background. Kawamura’s film adaptation, then, inherits this break condition and reinterprets it as a model for thinking through how the foreclosure of futurity under capitalist modernity might be overcome.

Returning to Caracciolo’s claim that the time loop “invite\[s\] readers to reflect on … how different the future might look depending on our personal and collective actions in the present” (2025, 154), it has become clear in the course of this essay that Kawamura’s *Exit 8* is similarly invested in futurity and turns its time loop toward these ends. What sets the film apart, however, is that it represents what remains in Caracciolo’s corpus only “the *prospect* of breaking out of the loop” (160, my emphasis) as a concrete narrative endpoint, charting a way toward futurity through an ethics of attentiveness from which emerges the possibility of plurality, action, and new beginnings. In this sense, *Exit 8* demonstrates that the ideological affordances of the time loop are to be found not only in its capacity to stage repetition, but also in the conditions it establishes for that repetition’s interruption—conditions through which escape from other forms of cyclical unfreedom becomes equally thinkable.

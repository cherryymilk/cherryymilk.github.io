William Shakespeare has a claim on the Weird. This may seem like just another gratuitously provocative suggestion amid the many competing, more or less eccentric candidates put forward as the genre’s forerunners—including Sheridan Le Fanu (Miéville 2009, 513), Mary Shelley (Mayer 2018), or even Homer (Weinstock 2016, 178)—but there is actually much precedent for it in criticism. It has become a favorite fact among scholars of the Weird that, through Old English wyrd, the word “weird” derives from Old Norse urðr, meaning “fate” or the “agency by which events are predetermined” (Oxford English Dictionary 2026; see also Claesby and Vigfusson 1957). In Norse mythology, Urðr is one of three Norns, alongside Verðandi and Skuld, who together “[m]ightily w[ea]ve … the web of fate” (Bellows 1923, 292). It is these goddesses who inspired Shakespeare’s “weïrd sisters” in Macbeth, with whom, according to Kerry Dodd, traces of the Weird’s modern meaning can first be glimpsed. Indeed, where wyrd had originally denoted “preordained … inevitable prophecies,” “weird” came to signal after Shakespeare an additional “degree of unknowability,” drawing the term toward the eerie, supernatural, and Other (2017, 42; see also Weinstock 2016, 177). These associations took further shape during the Romantic period, setting the stage for its fin-de-siècle emergence as a genre of fiction (Boletsi and Soudias 2025, 236; Alder 2020, 12).

Though the history of the term “weird” is typically told along these lines, as a narrative of semantic development and supersession, several critics have sought to lay bare the lasting presence of pre-Shakespearean wyrd as a component of the Weird rather than a ghost that occasionally rattles its antiquated chains at the genre. In The Weird and the Eerie, Mark Fisher observes the continued importance of fate to the workings of the Weird, arguing that “[t]he concept of fate is weird in that it implies twisted forms of … causality that are alien to ordinary perception” and “raises questions about agency: who or what is the entity that has woven fate?” (2016, 12). Jeffrey Weinstock has also emphasized that H. P. Lovecraft’s canonical definition of the Weird “weds the archaic understanding of wyrd as the external manipulation of one’s destiny with the contemporary comprehension of weird as absurd or bizarre” (2016, 179), while Dodd’s study of the semantic development of the term “weird” likewise acknowledges that the original meaning of wyrd, “whilst less overt, endures within the Weird and its cosmic determinism” (2017, 40). Though the genre evidently owes its name to the word’s more recent association with radical alterity and strangeness, these accounts suggest that the Weird has never fully shed this older preoccupation with fates woven by forces beyond human control.

But Shakespeare’s part in the history of the Weird points in another direction as well—one that, outside Shakespeare criticism, has rarely been acknowledged. It bears mentioning that, although the adjectival “weird” predates Macbeth, Shakespeare’s First Folio never actually applies this epithet to the three witches, opting instead for unique spellings such as weyrd, weyard, and weyward to emphasize the word’s two-syllable pronunciation. (Note also my use of diaeresis in my earlier mention of the “weïrd sisters.”) This is significant insofar as these spellings bring to mind “wayward,” another word that Shakespeare liked to use, which meant “self-willed” in the fourteenth century and had become “capriciously willful” by the sixteenth (Shamas 2007, 15–16). As Erik Davis has argued, this suggests that the three witches “derive their uncanny power not simply from their oracular knowledge but from their willful resistance to the norm” (2019, chap. 0.0.4). Shakespeare’s sisters are thus weird on three counts: as descendants of the Norns, they are agents of fate (“wyrd”); as witches, they embody the supernatural alterity associated with the word today (“weird”); and as wayward women, they are capricious and unbeholden to the dictates of others (“weyard”).

Yet where critics have traced the coexistence of wyrd and “weird” in the Weird, weyard has not yet been theorized alongside them. Roger Luckhurst recognizes that there is “a cluster of meanings that associates ‘weïrd’ … with ‘weyard,’” but uses this to color the Weird itself as a wayward genre “subject to chance, wrong turns, and disorientation,” and one that refuses “to fit narrative or generic expectation” (2017, 1049–50), rather than to explore the conceptual friction between waywardness and the other meanings latent in the genre’s name. Indeed, read together, wyrd and weyard pull in contrary directions: the former names a course that events are destined to follow, while the latter names a willful departure from any such course. That the Weird’s name should hold such contradictory impulses is, perhaps fittingly, “weird” in its own right.

To add another ingredient to its already overflowing cauldron of genre definitions, I would like to entertain with this essay the notion that one meaningful tendency of the Weird might lie precisely in this tension between determinism and waywardness. A clue to the forms this tension might assume in the genre emerges when we trace the etymology of “weird” one step further back. Old Norse urðr derives from Proto-Germanic *wurdi- (“fate, chance”), which in turn comes from Proto-Indo-European *wert-, meaning “to turn” or “to spin” (Kroonen 2013, 600; Kapović 2017, 33). At a fundamental level, then, “weird” is bound up with turning and spinning, akin to the Norns’ twining of the threads of fate. Though they do not directly cite this etymology, Timothy Morton invokes that same connection when they write that “[k]nowing in a loop” is “a weird knowing,” before describing the term “weird” as “a turn or twist or loop, a turn of events”(2016, 5). Nor is this affinity confined to semantics. Maria Boletsi and Dimitris Soudias intimate that this looping logic has made its way into the Weird as a genre, underlining its investment in “contingency and disorientation marked by ’twists’ and ‘loops’” (2025, 235–36).

Taking these suggestions literally, I propose that the loop is an imaginative form whose affordances are well-suited for accommodating the conceptual friction between wyrd and weyard. As Marco Caracciolo has argued, what sets apart the loop is that it “implies repetition and difference; it is an unstable form that always brings in the possibility of (more or less radical or disruptive) change” (2025, 152). By turning back on itself, the loop binds events to an unyielding recurrence, yet also admits variation and thus affords the possibility of wayward deviation. I contend that, when instantiated at the level of narrative (see Levine 2015), the loop thus becomes a form through which the Weird can stage its tension between determinism and waywardness. Though this instantiation can range widely, one meaningful case is what Wibke Schniedermann has termed the involuntary time loop (ITL) motif, in which the protagonist experiences a “continued repetition of a specific span of diegetic time separately from the external … timeline” (2023). It is this narrative form that I will consider in more detail in the remainder of this essay, using Genki Kawamura’s 2025 film Exit 8 as a case study.

I have elsewhere argued that Exit 8 uses the time loop to imagine how the foreclosure of futurity under capitalist modernity might be overcome (2026). Let me begin here by instead making the case that the film, to adopt Fisher’s terminology, possesses a “strong weird charge” (2016, 26). This charge manifests in two ways. First, on a predominantly aesthetic level, Exit 8 shares with the Weird its preoccupation with liminality. In his introduction to Lovecraft’s Classic Horror Stories, Luckhurst emphasizes that the Weird “concerns liminal things, in-between states, transgressions always on the verge of turning into something else” (2013, xvi). What is liminal in Exit 8 is the setting: an eerily deserted, fluorescent-lit subway corridor suspended in an infinite loop. This brings to mind the ‘Backrooms’ internet mythos and the popular concept of liminal spaces more generally (Khan 2026; Monagle 2026; see also Stephen 2022). Moreover, briefly appearing in the film are flesh-colored, rodent-like creatures whose bodies are similarly liminal: hairless, quadrupedal, and covered with human sensory organs (Kawamura 2025, 1:03:00). These resemble the Weird’s bizarre monsters, who constitute “a radical break with anything from a folkloric tradition” (Miéville 2009, 512).

More saliently, though, Exit 8’s weird charge can be located in its use of the time loop itself. Fisher’s suggestion that the Weird is tangled up with wyrd questions of causality and agency becomes highly relevant in this context, because in ITL narratives, protagonists have no conscious hand in starting the loop and remain ignorant as to “who or what controls [it] or why [they are] trapped in it” (Schniedermann 2023, 294). This is no less true for Exit 8, in which the protagonist, an unnamed young man commuting to work, finds himself trapped in the subway station seemingly out of nowhere, walking the same stretch of corridor over and over again. Escape from this loop is possible, as with most ITL narratives, provided that the protagonist satisfies a fixed set of terms: he must identify anomalies in his environment, in the style of a spot-the-difference puzzle. There is thus, to borrow Schniedermann’s terminology, an “inaccessible authority or higher power” (300) at play here, an eldritch agency whose identity and motivations remain entirely mysterious, even as the conditions it has ruled for his release are set in stone. This gives the loop a distinct logic of fate. The protagonist may not know what has imposed the loop or why, but he remains inescapably bound to its terms, bringing to mind Weinstock’s definition of the wyrd as “the supernatural power to manipulate the destiny of human beings” (2016, 177).

And yet, the loop’s determinism fails to impose a totalizing regime. To echo Caracciolo, it is an “unstable” form: unruly and, indeed, wayward. There are two significant levels on which waywardness enters the logic of the loop. First, Exit 8’s time loop is designed to be imperfect from the outset. The very premise of the film—and the indie video game it is inspired by, Kotake Create’s The Exit 8 (2023)—relies upon the presence of subtle deviations in the loop, styled as “anomalies.” When the protagonist first enters the loop, he notices a sign posted on the wall beside him that reads: “Do not overlook any anomalies. / If you find an anomaly, turn back immediately. / If you do not find any anomalies, do not turn back. / Go out from Exit 8” (0:13:20). These anomalies soon become apparent as he moves through different iterations of the passage, encountering not only minor changes like altered posters, but also major phenomena that threaten his safety, such as a deluge of brownish liquid that floods the corridor without warning (1:14:35). In effect, every reset of the loop is thus revealed to be potentially deficient, concretizing the “pattern of difference within repetition” that, according to Caracciolo, defines the loop as a form (2025, 154). What this means is that waywardness is written into the loop’s very architecture, each iteration allowing the corridor to “veer” from its previous form, to recall Luckhurst.

But waywardness also inheres in the protagonist’s passage through the loop. Although the terms of his escape have been fixed in advance by powers beyond his understanding, his actions within this structure have not. After reading the instructions mounted on the wall beside him, the protagonist retains the freedom to wander aimlessly, stand in place for hours, escape within minutes or spend months in the loop. In the film, he does not immediately submit to the order imposed upon him. He instead veers from its demands, making numerous unsuccessful attempts at getting around them, for instance by photographing the corridor instead of attending directly to its anomalies. Importantly, though, this waywardness ends up working in favor of the loop’s terms in Exit 8. Schniedermann et al. have noted that most time loops behave like feedback loops, feeding knowledge gained during one iteration into the next. More concretely, this implies that looping characters “will almost inevitably act differently and make different choices with each iteration … until it ultimately results in a different outcome” (2025, 4). This is precisely what happens in the film, as the protagonist learns from his mistakes and successfully escapes the loop in the end. It is only through his veering, then, that he learns to satisfy the predetermined terms of his escape.

Taken together, its intentionally imperfect design and the protagonist’s veering path toward escape suggest that Exit 8’s ITL paradoxically enacts its determinism through waywardness. It deviates from its previous form with each iteration, producing the anomalies on which its telos depends, while the protagonist veers in response, each misstep drawing him closer to that telos. The loop can thus be said to bind wyrd and weyard at the level of narrative: its endpoint is determined by an obscure agency that resembles fate, though the route toward it goes off course at every turn. Consequently, Exit 8 becomes a work of the Weird in each of the three senses I have discussed: “weird” in its liminal aesthetics, wyrd in the preordained design of its loop, and weyard in the veering path that answers to it.

Yet this copresence of wyrd and weyard remains uneasy, as neither logic yields fully to the other. The loop does not reconcile them so much as hold them in suspension. Watching the protagonist of Exit 8 stumble his way through the loop, one cannot help but ask: is his waywardness truly just the enactment of a fate already imposed upon him, or is that fate reliant on choices that are genuinely his own? The protagonist’s veering appears to have been exactly the route by which the loop's preordained design was realized, yet this looks inevitable only in retrospect. At each juncture, he might have chosen to curl into a ball and stop trying altogether. This is a possibility the loop never forecloses, even as its telos, once fulfilled, makes the protagonist’s perseverance appear inexorable all along. The film offers no clear way to adjudicate between these possibilities, and this is where the weirdness of the loop truly surfaces: not merely from its formal accommodation of both wyrd and weyard, but rather from its sustention of their philosophical irresolution.

And this is a friction that reaches beyond the loop to reopen and reconfigure the Weird’s perennial concern with the intentions of eldritch unknowns “situated in a wider, cosmic understanding outside human comprehension” (Dodd 2017, 41). For if the fate imposed by the loop depends upon the protagonist’s waywardness for its fulfillment, this invites speculation, to echo Fisher, about the “entity that has woven” it (2016, 12). In Exit 8, the instructions posted on the corridor wall provide the only clues to that entity’s designs: the protagonist must earn his release by becoming more attentive to his surroundings. Read alongside the film’s opening scene, in which he chooses to ignore a young mother’s harassment on the subway, this demand acquires an unexpectedly ethical dimension. Indeed, the loop forces the protagonist to reckon with and work through his self-serving attentional priorities as he navigates the corridor (see Ballegeer 2026 for a more detailed discussion). Upon his escape, then, he returns to the same moment on the subway and this time, having gone through the loop, chooses to stand up for the woman. 

The “higher power” behind the loop is therefore a peculiar one for two reasons. First, this agency seems to have traded the cosmic indifference one would typically expect from the Weird for an interest in human morality. It takes on a corrective role that appears, in stark contrast to the early Lovecraftian Weird, almost benevolent, favoring collectivist action over the protagonist’s initial individualism. Second, for all its power to bend the laws of space and time, this agency cannot directly compel the choices on which its design depends, remaining entirely reliant upon the protagonist’s waywardness. For this reason, it has recourse to strategies of coercion in the form of horrific and life-threatening anomalies, throwing into question this apparent benevolence while simultaneously revealing the boundaries of its influence to be finite and, crucially, strangely knowable. In Exit 8, then, the friction between wyrd and weyard sustained by the time loop can be said to dethrone the Weird’s cosmic unknowns, recasting them as bounded powers invested in human affairs.

To return to where this essay began: Shakespeare has a claim on the Weird by virtue of his three “weïrd sisters,” whose name first knit together the bizarre, the fated, and the wayward—three qualities that this essay has shown coexist and collide in the Weird. Of wyrd, “weird,” and weyard, it is the latter whose centrality to the workings of the genre has gone largely neglected in criticism. The loop, I have argued in my analysis of Exit 8, is one form through which the continued grip of weyard on the Weird can be seen at work on the level of narrative, binding repetition to difference in a way that holds determinism and waywardness together without one ever quite resolving into the other. Thus, if Shakespeare’s witches first showed that the Weird could be fated and wayward at once, Exit 8’s use of the time loop shows that the genre has never stopped finding new forms in which to stage that same, unresolved inheritance.

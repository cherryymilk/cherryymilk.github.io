### Introduction

Podcast horror has enjoyed unprecedented popularity in recent years. In 2017, Danielle Hancock and Leslie McMurtry observed that, following the international success of Joseph Fink and Jeffrey Cranor’s *Welcome to Night Vale* (2012–present), the genre increasingly saw “wide circulation, critical acclaim and popular appeal” (2017, 2). This has not abated in the intervening decade, with series like *The NoSleep Podcast* (2011–), *Lore* (2015–), *The Black Tapes* (2015–2017), *Archive 81* (2016–2019), *Old Gods of Appalachia* (2019–), *The Lovecraft Investigations* (2019–), and *CreepCast* (2023–) amassing large (cult) followings. Data pulled from Google Trends show a sharply rising interest in the search queries “horror podcast” and “audio horror” since the summer of 2025 (see figure below). That same year, the supernatural horror podcast *The Magnus Archives* (2016–2021), written by Jonathan Sims and directed by Alexander J. Newall, became one of the first British podcasts to surpass 100 million downloads on Spotify (Rusty Quill 2025). In brief, podcast horror is on the rise, and this is a development that has begun to draw the attention of literary and cultural critics. Existing work has largely situated the genre within the Gothic, examining how the medial affordances of podcasting (e.g., binaural recording) contribute to sensory horror (Euritt and McMurtry 2021), how horror podcasts like *The Magnus Archives*, *Archive 81*, and *The Black Tapes* remediate analog recording technologies as sources of horror (Álvarez Trigo 2024; Frampton-Thorburn 2026; Malvestio 2025), and how *The Magnus Archives* challenges the Gothic association of queerness with transgression (Juko 2024).

Hancock and McMurtry observe that podcast horror is a composite category that encompasses a wide range of audiophonic narratives, including “campfire-style oral tales,” “audio dramas reminiscent of radio’s Golden Era,” and “dramas delivered in radio-broadcast style” (2017, 1). Still, critics have paid surprisingly little attention to the genre’s heterogeneity. From a formal perspective, these works in fact adopt distinct modes of narrative mediation that the term “podcast horror” flattens, and that deserve to be untangled. One productive way of approaching these differences is through the distinction between *diegetic* and *mimetic* modes—that is, between narration and enactment (see Ryan 2021; Nünning and Sommer 2008). Following Marie-Laure Ryan’s call to examine “how diegetic narration can complement the mimetic mode and overcome its limitations” in audiophonic narrative (2021, 216), this essay takes *The Magnus Archives* as a case study, arguing that it brings these two modes together in a distinctive and self-reflexive way. Specifically, I contend that the podcast places extended acts of narration at the center of its episodes while gradually developing a frame narrative mediated through enactment around them, so that the balance between narration and enactment changes over the course of the series. The series further dramatizes the conditions of its own mediation in the storyworld, as characters grow increasingly aware of and lose control over the tape recorders that function as diegetic surrogates for its “audiophonic composition device” (Bluijs 2021, 88). By attending to narrative mediation, then, *The Magnus Archives* emerges as a distinctive generic blend of anthology fiction and radio plays. My analysis thus draws out a formal specificity that is easily lost under the umbrella of “podcast horror.”

In what follows, I first examine the formal interplay between mimetic and diegetic mediation in *The Magnus Archives*, using the early episodes “Do Not Open” and “The Boneturner’s Tale” as case studies. I also compare these cases with a later episode in the series, “Family Business” (Season 3), to demonstrate the growing salience of the mimetic frame narrative relative to the diegetic acts of narration around which the podcast is initially organized. Finally, I consider the implications of this formal organization and its development for the narrative itself, before concluding with a discussion of its stakes in relation to the broader generic category of podcast horror.

### Telling Frames and Story Frames

At the outset of *The Magnus Archives*, each episode has at its center an act of diegetic narration—i.e., the mediation of narrative information “through the discourse of a narrator” (Ryan 2021, 215). The series’ premiere, “Angler Fish,” opens with the protagonist, Jonathan Sims (“Jon”), recording himself on tape to describe his recent employment as head archivist at the *Magnus Institute*, “an organisation dedicated to academic research into the esoteric and the paranormal.” Jon is tasked with organizing an impossibly messy archive filled with the written testimonies (“statements”) of people who have had encounters with the supernatural. He describes his course of action as follows: “I plan to digitise the files as much as possible and record audio versions, though some will have to be on tape recorder … \[My assistants\] will be doing some supplementary investigation to see what details may be missing from what we have. I’ll try to present these in as succinct a fashion as I can at the end of each statement” (Rusty Quill 2016a, 1–3)[^1]. This accurately describes how the early episodes of the podcast are structured: Jon introduces a statement, reads it aloud for the recording, and elaborates on his assistants’ additional investigative efforts at the end.

Jon spends most of the episode “Do Not Open” narrating the statement of a young man named Joshua Gillespie, who experienced strange phenomena in 1998 during his time “in possession of an apparently empty wooden casket” (2016b, 1). Crucially, these events reach the listener only through a nested structure of diegetic mediation. Jon reads aloud and records on tape Joshua’s experiences, which the latter first narrated nearly two decades prior in the form of a written statement. What the listener hears, then, is not Joshua’s encounter enacted as he might have experienced it, but Jon’s vocal narration of Joshua’s written narration of said encounter. This renders the listener’s narrative access to these events entirely contingent upon the tape recorder. As Lowen Frampton-Thorburn has observed, “while there is a diegetic reason for the audio being recorded—digitising the statements—there is no clear explanation as to how the audience is able to hear this …; spatially, the audience’s ears are *inside* the tape player, hearing only what it hears” (2026, 4). As such, the tape recorder occupies an unusual position between narrative levels: it is an archival device that Jon uses to record statements within the storyworld, yet concurrently a diegetic surrogate for what, following Siebe Bluijs, might be called the podcast’s “audiophonic composition device,” a theoretical agency that “narrates by arranging and composing sonic layers and auditory elements” (2021, 88) and thus determines what the listener can or cannot hear.

Jon’s diegetic act of narration thus becomes responsible for mediating the listener’s access not only to Joshua’s tale, but also to the wider storyworld in which his telling takes place. At the end of “Do Not Open,” when Jon has finished narrating, he quips that it is “always nice to hear that \[his\] hometown \[Bournemouth\] is not entirely devoid of odd occurrences and eerie stories” (11). Similarly, he mentions having spoken to one of his colleagues, Tim, about the case (12). These moments are significant insofar as they capture information independent from Joshua’s statement and make visible (or audible) the contours of a diegesis beyond it. In this episode, this information, too, becomes accessible only through diegetic mediation. Everything the listener learns with respect to what Jon feels, whom he has spoken to, and what he has learned issues from his own narration into the tape recorder. A storyworld thus begins to surface, but one bounded within what Ansgar Nünning and Roy Sommer call the “telling frame” rather than within the “story frame” (2008, 339; see also Fludernik 1996). The diegesis in *The Magnus Archives* remains, in other words, purely an object of Jon’s discourse.

As the series progresses, however, moments of mimetic mediation increasingly come to punctuate episodes. Nünning and Sommer define the mimetic mode as a “performative … transmission of narrative content” that, in contrast to the diegetic mode, does not “presuppose the presence of a speaker, a proposition, a communicative situation, an addressee or a recipient role” (2008, 338). As such, it is a mode of mediation that seeks to disguise the very fact of mediation, producing, according to Lars Bernaerts, an “illusion of immediacy” (2021, 65). In the episode “The Boneturner’s Tale,” Jon reads a statement by Sebastian Adekoya, a librarian employed at Chiswick Library. Midway through his narration, Jon’s employer, Elias Bouchard, enters the room and interrupts him, letting him know that somebody has “lodged a complaint” against him (2016c, 5). What follows is a brief dialogue between the two characters, mediated to the listener through mimetic enactment. When Elias leaves the room, Jon states, “Statement resumes” (6), and continues his narration of Sebastian’s testimony. The same tape recorder that mediates the “telling frame” thus momentarily comes to mediate the “story frame” as well, granting the listener access to an event that occurs independent of Jon’s narration.

This shift in mediation matters because it affords a fuller “look” at the storyworld (to borrow a visual metaphor for lack of a better auditory one). Here, this primarily takes the form of dialogue, which, according to Bernaerts, “fulfill\[s\] a mimetic function” in audiophonic narrative (2017, 220). Features of the voice like intonation, pitch, and stress communicate “dramatic, psychological, and narrative meaning” (209), while fictional dialogic episodes in particular tend to “concentrate narrative information” (217). This information need no longer pass through Jon’s perception, interpretation, and reformulation before entering the recording and thus reaching the listener. In “The Boneturner’s Tale,” Elias’s interruption only briefly cuts short the episode’s otherwise consistent diegetic mediation, shedding light on specific details like the uneasy rapport between Jon and his superior, as well as the Institute’s dealings with wealthy donors. As *The Magnus Archives* progresses, however, episodes have growing recourse to mimetic mediation as the “story frame” of the events unfolding around Jon and his colleagues grows in salience relative to the “telling frame” of Jon’s statement narration. Indeed, as Frampton-Thorburn has observed, these characters are increasingly “drawn into an overarching plot about horrors emerging from the stories on the tapes” (2026, 2). There is, in other words, a “meta-plot” that develops “\[a\]round and within the individual stories on which each episode pivots” (Malvestio 2025, 66), and much of this plot unfolds through mimetic enactment.

This altered balance is on display particularly clearly in the season three episode “Family Business.” The episode opens not with Jon’s usual routine of introducing and reading a statement from the archive, but rather with a conversation (recorded on tape) between himself and two characters who regularly feature in these statements, Julia Montauk and Trevor Herbert. From them, Jon acquires a supernatural book that harbors the consciousness of Gerard Keay, a deceased assistant to Jon’s predecessor as head archivist of the Magnus Institute. Jon and Gerard enter into an extended dialogue about matters central to the developing meta-plot, and only well into their conversation does Jon suggest: “Do you… want to make a statement?” (2018, 12). Here, Gerard’s diegetic narration of his experiences no longer structures the episode as a whole, but rather makes up one discrete “telling frame” within a “story frame” that was already unfolding prior to it and resumes once the statement ends. The relation between these two modes has thus effectively reversed from “The Boneturner’s Tale,” as diegetic mediation now temporarily interrupts mimetic mediation rather than vice versa. And this structure grows ever more common as the series progresses: by the later seasons, numerous episodes dispense with statements entirely (e.g., “Recollection” \[2020a\]), and a select few contain almost no diegetic mediation at all (e.g., “Panopticon” \[2019\]). Taken together, these examples show that, while diegetic mediation remains prominent throughout the majority of *The Magnus Archives*, mimetic mediation gradually comes to occupy a greater share of the narrative as the series progresses.

### Mediation and Metanarrative

This shifting narrative mediation has important implications that stretch beyond the podcast’s discourse level and toward the story itself. Because the tape recorder is, as established, responsible for mediating the listener’s access to all acts of narration and enactment, the growing salience of the latter places increasing strain on the diegetic rationale for the device’s presence. In “Do Not Open,” the tape recorder serves a clear purpose. The episode begins, as always, “with the click of \[it\] turning on” (Frampton-Thorburn 2026, 9), followed by Jon introducing the statement: “Statement of Joshua Gillespie …. Original statement given November 22nd 1998. Audio recording by Jonathan Sims, Head Archivist of the Magnus Institute, London” (1). Similarly, after Jon has provided additional details of the investigation, the episode ends with the sound of a tape recorder turning off. It is evident that Jon deliberately switches on and off the tape recorder, using it specifically to carry out his responsibilities as archivist. Recording is thus motivated at the diegetic level entirely by Jon’s acts of narration. So long as the podcast is organized around statement recording, then, the tape recorder’s presence requires no further explanation.

As the “story frame” expands, however, the podcast begins to stage events that the tape recorder has no clear diegetic reason to record. And yet, because the listener’s access is confined entirely to what the device captures, it must somehow be present if these events are to remain audible. The series accounts for this problem by making the tape recorder “alive” in the story: a haunted, analog medium that possesses “a sinister agency” (Malvestio 2025, 75). As Frampton-Thorburn observes, the tape recorders slowly begin “appearing and turning on of their own accord,” capturing interactions and events “with the voyeuristic eye of surveillance” and without characters’ knowledge or consent (2026, 9). To revisit one of my earlier cases, “Family Business” opens in the middle of a conversation between Jon, Julia, and Trevor, none of whom acknowledge the tape recorder in their dialogue. This strongly suggests that these characters did not themselves turn on the device and are indeed unaware of its presence. The tape recorder thus develops from a device designed for diegetic mediation to what Ryan describes as “a ghostlike presence” with an agency of its own (2021, 219), capable of mediating the “story frame” without being anchored to a “telling frame.”

Crucially, then, it is not only narrative mediation itself that is dramatized at the diegetic level by means of the tape recorder, but also its transformation throughout the series. And this opens the door for characters within the storyworld to notice, interpret, and comment on the podcast’s very mediating apparatus in distinctly metanarrative gestures (see Nünning 2005, 16). The season five episode “Ignorance,” for instance, features the following interaction between Jon, his assistant Martin Blackwood, and a Samoan antiquities trafficker called Mikaele Salesa (2020b, 5):

> MIKAELE. Now tell me, do you know why there’s a tape recorder here? I noticed it just now, but I don’t believe I actually own one.

> ARCHIVIST. Uh... Not really.

> MARTIN. They sort of just … follow us round.

> MIKAELE. Hmmmm. Interesting. … Things shouldn’t be able to manifest in here like that. … Very well. In that case, we shall leave it to be. It’s hardly valuable, and it’s probably best not to upset whatever it might be involved with. Besides, I have no secrets to hide.

Martin’s observation that the tape recorders “follow \[them\] around” describes, at the diegetic level, simply their supernatural mobility; yet at the discourse level, it alludes to the narrative’s persistent need for a mediating instance to track mimetic enactment (and implicates the listener as the beneficiary of that mobility). What becomes visible on this account is that the tape recorder gives material form to precisely this mediating agency, one that, according to Bernaerts, does not actually “have to be … embodied by a distinct entity (either human or nonhuman)” (2021, 78). By embodying that agency regardless, *The Magnus Archives* dramatizes (and even self-reflexively comments on) the apparatus on which mimetic mediation depends, which allows the podcast to expose its “illusion of immediacy” (65) as an effect of mediation rather than of its absence.

### Conclusion

With this essay, I have demonstrated that *The Magnus Archives* mediates its narrative predominantly through diegetic narration in the form of “statements” recorded on tape, and that these statements gradually give way to an increasingly salient frame narrative marked by mimetic mediation. Moreover, I have shown that this formal development is itself dramatized and thematized in the diegesis, where tape recorders come to materialize the discursive agency behind mimetic mediation. By way of conclusion, I briefly bring these observations to bear on the podcast’s generic positioning. On this account, the distinctive narrative mediation of *The Magnus Archives* places it between two established genres of audiophonic horror fiction. On the one hand, its central acts of statement narration align the series with anthology horror fiction podcasts such as *The NoSleep Podcast*, *Pseudopod* (2006–), and *The Drabblecast* (2007–), which themselves remediate the printed horror anthology genre by vocally transmitting supernatural and uncanny tales through diegetic mediation (see Bolter and Grusin 1999). On the other hand, the series’ frame narrative draws on the mimetic mediation of horror radio serials like *We’re Alive* (2009–2014), *The Lovecraft Investigations*, and *Malevolent* (2020–), themselves “import\[ing\] the conventions of drama” (Bernaerts 2021, 75). Because mediation shifts over the course of the series, the generic identity of *The Magnus Archives* begins closer to the anthology podcast and gradually drifts toward the radio play.

The entanglement of diegetic and mimetic mediation is, of course, not unique to *The Magnus Archives*. Podcast horror (like audiophonic narrative more generally) tends to combine narration and enactment in varying ways. Hancock and McMurtry argue that the nonfiction podcast *Serial* (2014–) has “undeniably influenced a new form of fictive horror podcasting” (2017, 6) in recent years, one featuring what Laura Álvarez Trigo describes as “investigative journalism stories with a mix of prerecorded sections and a narrator providing context to the shared bits” (2024, 194). Podcasts like *Archive 81* and *The Black Tapes* adopt precisely this structure, organizing their episodes around mimetic material like interviews, conversations, and other scenes recorded on tape, and supplementing this with diegetic commentary by a host or narrator. It is tempting to place *The Magnus Archives* within this same “post-*Serial*” tradition, not least because scholars have tended to discuss the series in conjunction with the above podcasts, and because the series’ early seasons “show a significant debt to *Serial*” (Malvestio 2025, 70; see also Frampton-Thorburn 2026). However, as my analysis has brought to light, the series adopts an almost inverse relation between narration and enactment, centering diegetic mediation itself rather than using it to contextualize material presented through mimetic mediation. On this view, *The Magnus Archives* exemplifies Hancock and McMurtry’s claim that today’s productions meaningfully “expand upon *Serial’s blueprint*” in varying ways (2018, 83).

More broadly, this essay’s attention to narrative mediation has revealed that the category of “podcast horror” harbors and obscures substantial formal heterogeneity, at once accommodating works aligned with anthology fiction, radio drama, and generic hybrids like *The Magnus Archives*. If, as Frampton-Thorburn argues, horror podcasts “deliberately use their medium in such a way that differentiates them as something new and individual as opposed to simply a digitised portable radio drama or oral narrative” (2026, 2), my analysis suggests that one such site of differentiation lies in how they recombine inherited conventions of narrative mediation. Attending to this negotiation brings into view salient features that the umbrella of “podcast horror” tends to flatten.
